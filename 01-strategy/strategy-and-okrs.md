# Product Strategy One-Pager & OKRs: [Fable / Meridian / your initiative]

> Module 1 · Craft an Advanced Product Strategy, ★ Deliverable 1
>
> Your one spine: the **Playing to Win** cascade, one deliberate **hard no**, and an **OKR cascade** that flows directly from it.
> Draft the cascade + hard no in Sprint 1, then add the OKRs in Sprint 2. The goal: specific enough that a skeptical board member couldn't poke a hole in it.

## 0. Chosen scenario

**Path:** _Fable Growth (B2C · retention + engagement) · Meridian Foundations (B2B · adoption + expansion) · my own initiative_

Fable Growth, because it's a retention crisis born from the product's own success — a sharper strategic problem to work through than a straightforward growth or acquisition gap.

## 1. Playing to Win cascade

| Question | Your choice |
|---|---|
| **Winning aspiration**: winning in the customer's terms, not internal metrics | The app I turn to for ongoing support long after my original problem eased, not just for the intense first few months. |
| **Where to play**: segment, geography, channel, use case (the no's matter too) | Adults 25–45 who already resolved their acute need on Fable in their first 90–120 days (the existing base at risk of going quiet) — not new-user acquisition, not a broader age band, not a clinical/diagnostic use case. |
| **How to win**: your differentiator competitors can't easily replicate | Sustaining ongoing engagement through the calm, non-clinical experience itself, rather than through health-vitals tracking or intensity/brand-awareness plays that Headspace, Calm, and AI-native rivals compete on. |
| **Capabilities required**: what you must be world-class at (build / buy / partner) | Build a usage/behavioral analytics capability to detect where a user sits in their journey (acute vs. post-acute) and when they enter the 90–120 day drop-off window, feeding an adaptive content/personalization engine that serves different content for maintenance than for acute need — both built in-house, since they extend the core content IP rather than bolt on a new category. |
| **Management systems**: the metrics and rituals that reinforce your choices | 90-day+ retention rate, day-91–120 reopen/reactivation rate — the specific metric named in the crisis itself. |

## 2. Your one hard no

_One valuable thing you are explicitly choosing **not** to do, and why it protects the focus of everything above. This is a deliberate trade-off, not a backlog of deprioritized items._

> We will not build health-vitals tracking or clinical-monitoring features into Fable because it would reposition the app as a clinical platform, undermining the trust and simplicity the core calming experience was built on.

## 3. OKR cascade

_One Objective and three Key Results that flow directly from the cascade. Each KR must be a measurable **outcome**, not an output/milestone._

> **Objective:** Turn Fable from an acute-need app into a durable companion users keep opening after their original problem resolves.
>
> - **KR1:** 90-day+ retention rate from 15% to 30% by Q4 2026
> - **KR2:** Day 91–120 reopen/reactivation rate from 8% to 20% by Q4 2026
> - **KR3:** 180-day retention rate from 10% to 22% by Q4 2026

## 4. AI pressure-test notes

_Run the devil's-advocate prompt (in the Sprint 2 guide). Capture the verdict._

| Prompt question | What the AI surfaced | Change or defend? |
|---|---|---|
| Biggest assumption that could be wrong | That sustaining engagement through the existing calm, non-clinical content (via personalization/analytics) is enough to keep users past day 90–120 — versus users needing something genuinely new once their acute need is resolved. The brief confirms the core content solves the acute need; it says nothing about whether that same content style can hold attention once the need is gone. This is the central, untested bet behind "how to win." | Defend for now, but flag as the riskiest unproven assumption — this is exactly what the prototype (Deliverable 3) should be built to test before committing further investment. |
| The board question I can't yet answer | "What's our actual current 90-day retention rate, and how severe is the drop-off?" The KR baselines (15%, 8%, 10%) are assumed placeholders, not real data — a board would ask where we're starting from before approving targets. | Change — before this goes to a board, the assumed numbers need to be replaced with real baseline data. |
| KRs that are outputs in disguise | None of the three are outputs — all are retention-rate outcomes, not features/launches. The real risk is redundancy, not disguise: KR1 (90-day retention) and KR3 (180-day retention) will likely move together, so they may not be fully independent signals of success. | Defend the KRs as outcomes; watch for correlation between KR1 and KR3 once real data exists. |
| The "no" I should reconsider | The no-vitals-tracking stance is the one most likely to be pressure-tested by a board, since AI-native rivals may differentiate exactly there. But the brief states this constraint explicitly ("without repositioning as a clinical platform") — it isn't my call to reconsider, it's a given boundary. | Defend — this no is anchored to an explicit constraint in the brief, not a discretionary choice. |
| Strategy or wish list? Why? | It's a strategy, not a wish list — it makes one falsifiable bet (personalization/analytics on existing content, not new content or vitals tracking), states a real trade-off (giving up the vitals-tracking differentiation path), and ties to measurable outcomes. It's incomplete only in that the KR baselines are still assumed rather than real. | · |
